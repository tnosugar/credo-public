State law · varies

How long a debt can be sued on.

The statute of limitations is the deadline for a creditor or collector to sue you over a debt. It varies by state and by the type of debt. Once it passes, the debt still exists, but a court can no longer be used to force you to pay, which makes an expired statute one of the strongest defenses in a collection lawsuit.

What it is

The statute of limitations sets a deadline on lawsuits, not on the debt itself. Once the period runs, a creditor or collector can no longer win a court judgment forcing you to pay, though they may still ask you to pay and the debt may still appear on your credit report for a separate window.

The length of the period is set by state law and depends on the type of debt. A written contract, an oral agreement, and an open account like a credit card can each carry a different period within the same state. When the clock starts (the date of last payment, default, or charge-off) can itself be a legal question, so treat any single figure as a general starting point rather than a certainty.

The two things to know

How the clock restarts, and how the deadline works as a defense.

The plain explanation comes first; the example shows what it looks like in practice.

Re-aging · restarting the clock

A single payment or written promise can restart the clock from zero.

In many states, certain actions reset the statute of limitations, turning a nearly expired debt back into one a collector can sue on. The most common triggers are making a payment (even a small "good-faith" one), acknowledging the debt in writing, or agreeing to a new payment plan. Collectors sometimes ask for exactly this, framed as cooperation. On an old debt, the safe rule is to pay nothing and admit nothing until you have confirmed where the statute stands.

Example

A collector pursues you over a personal loan you stopped paying almost the full limitations period ago, and presses for a $20 payment "to show good faith." In a state where payment re-ages the debt, that single $20 can restart the clock and hand the collector years of new time to sue.

Time-barred · affirmative defense

An expired statute is a defense you have to raise, and the debt still exists.

If a collector sues on a time-barred debt, a judge does not throw the case out automatically. You have to raise the expired statute as an affirmative defense in your written answer, within the deadline to respond. Miss that deadline and a winnable case can become a default judgment. The debt is not erased either: it can still be requested and can still appear on your credit report for a separate window (generally about seven years under the FCRA). Suing on a debt the collector knows is time-barred can itself violate the FDCPA §1692e.

Example

You are served over a credit-card balance that went to default well beyond your state's period. If you file an answer that raises the expired statute, the collector generally cannot win. If you ignore the summons, the court can enter a default judgment against you even though the debt was time-barred.
